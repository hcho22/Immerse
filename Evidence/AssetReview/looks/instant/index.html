Format Reference
The original 1970s Polaroid integral print (SX-70 film), as it looked fresh from the camera
PRD 2.1 section 6
Square picture on a white card that is always part of the print. Brilliant, warm, saturated color and soft detail, with a slight variation in color and density from print to print. No uneven-chemistry or spread flaws. The contrast and the size of the variation wait on a study of preserved 1970s prints.
What to judge
  • Color: the faded look approved on 2026-10-10: muted and warm, with blue in the shadows and yellow in the highlights.
  • Contrast: how deep the blacks go, how bright the whites are, and how far the middle tones spread between them.
  • Variation: how much one print of a pack differs from the next in brightness and in color; never a flaw.
  • Detail: soft, with a coarse texture.

What the study of preserved prints found

The study measured 37 scans of prints made between 1973 and 1979. All are openly licensed and are shown at the foot of this board with their sources. It weighed three things: what the standard reference on print permanence says these prints do with age, what a scanner does to a print, and what the scans themselves show.

Film, age and scanner. The reference (Wilhelm and Brower, The Permanence and Care of Color Photographs, 1993) found the dyes of these prints "extremely stable in dark storage": a print kept in an album keeps its color and its contrast. What age adds is an overall yellowish stain, plainest in the whites, and fine cracks under the surface of early prints. A scanner changes more than age does. In 26 of the 37 scans the blacks are crushed to nothing or the whites burned out, by the scanner or by later editing, and those scans cannot say where a print's black or white sits.

What the 11 usable scans show. The blacks of preserved prints are deeper than the app makes them, the whites sit a little below the white card as the app has them, the middle tones spread further apart, and the color is about twice as strong. Preserved prints are less flat than the contrast the Instant had before the study.

What the scans cannot show. No scan is of a known scene, so the exact shape of the film's curve cannot be read from them, only where its two ends sit and how far the tones spread between them. Only three usable scans include the white card, so the black and white figures rest on three prints. The contrast the captain chose moves the app toward the scans on every measure and stops short of them on each.

Print to print. The best evidence is three sets of prints one photographer made in single evenings in 1976, 1978 and 1979. Four prints of the 1978 evening were scanned without adjustment, and they differ from one another more than twice as much in brightness as ten prints do in the app, and about one and a half times as much in color. Each of the four is of a different scene, so those are upper limits. The board showed a variation widened to match them beside the one the app had, and the captain kept the one the app had. Prints of different evenings differ far more, with green blacks one year and purple the next, but that is different batches of film, decades of different storage and different scanning sessions, not one pack.

The contrast before and after the study

In the app (treatment version 4)

The contrast the captain chose on 2026-10-13, B on this board when it was served to him. The same faded color, grain, softness and white card as before. Black sits a little deeper, at 11 percent of the tone scale, and white stays at 90. Between them is the S-shaped curve of a print: steeper through the middle tones and easing into black and into white.

Before the study (treatment version 3)

The look he approved on 2026-10-10 with the contrast it had until the study, A on this board when it was served. Its curve is flatter than the phone's own: it opens the shadows and holds the middle tones close together. Black sits at 13 percent of the tone scale and white at 90. An Instant Film loaded under version 3 still develops this way.

    /// 1970s Instant. Format Reference: the original 1970s integral print. By the captain's choice on its review board
    /// on 2026-10-10 ("faded"), it develops as surviving prints look, not as fresh ones did (ADR 0016, an exception to
    /// ADR 0013 and to PRD 2.1 section 6.1's "brilliant, warm, saturated color"): lifted, milky blacks, softened
    /// whites, muted color with aqua blues and olive greens, blue in the shadows and yellow in the highlights, soft
    /// detail and a coarse texture. Each print still differs slightly in color and density, with no uneven-chemistry
    /// or spread flaws.
    ///
    /// Its contrast is the one the captain chose on 2026-10-13 ("B") from the study of 37 preserved 1970s prints
    /// (`Evidence/NativeApp/instant-study-and-toning-061.md`): black a little deeper than before, white where it
    /// was, and between them the S-shaped curve of a print, steeper through the middle tones and easing into black
    /// and into white, where the look he approved on 2026-10-10 held the middle tones flat. Measured as the scans
    /// were, its prints sit closer to them in black, white, the spread of their tones and the strength of their color,
    /// and short of them in each. The scans cannot give the film's curve itself, only its ends and its spread.
    static let instant = Treatment(
        emulsion: .color(ColorEmulsion(
            warmth: 0.09, tint: -0.005, layers: Layers(redFromGreen: 0.12, greenFromBlue: 0.15, blueFromGreen: 0.15),
            saturation: 0.85,
            tone: ToneCurve(contrast: 1.3, midtones: 0.9, black: 0.11, white: 0.9),
            shadows: RGB(0, 0.008, 0.03), highlights: RGB(0.012, 0.004, -0.03),
            grain: Grain(amplitude: 4.5, size: 0.0014, chroma: 0.3)
        )),
        lens: Lens(softness: 0.0011, edgeSoftness: 0.001, edgeDarkening: 0.4),
        printVariation: PrintVariation(density: 0.12, warmth: 0.025, tint: 0.01)
    )
